# Atsumaru: A Design Review

*On the website at atsu.moe, as it appeared to a logged-out visitor on a desktop screen, 13 October 2026. Rankings and timestamps on the site change by the minute; the design does not.*

---

## 1. The first ten seconds

You arrive and nothing asks anything of you.

The screen is a deep, soft charcoal, the kind of dark that reads as a dimmed room rather than a void. Across it, twelve book covers sit in two rows of six, each a tall portrait with gently rounded corners, each one blazing with its own colour: a green football pitch, a turquoise sky, a girl in a sailor collar against white, a purple-lit swordsman. The covers supply every ounce of colour on the page. Everything the site itself drew is grey, white, or a hush of lilac.

Above the grid, a single heading in light, unhurried type: **Hot Updates**, with a small chevron beside it like a door left ajar. Above that, a slim bar: the word ATSUMARU in heavy capitals at the left, two small icons, a wide rounded search field dead centre with a faint two-key shortcut printed inside it, and at the far right a handful of icons and two plain underlined words, Login and Sign up. That is the entire frame. No banner. No slogan. No hero photograph of a smiling person. No countdown, no cookie wall, no video.

What you understand, before touching anything, is three things. First: this is a library, and a large one, because the covers are clearly real published series and not stock art. Second: it is alive right now, because under each title a small dim line says "24m ago", "27m ago", "45m ago", and the numbers are different for each. Third: it is not going to sell you anything, because nothing on the screen is shaped like a pitch. The page has the posture of a well-run shop whose owner nodded at you and went back to shelving.

What you feel is calm, and then, a half-second later, appetite. The calm comes from the greys: the interface is so quiet that your eye slides straight off it and onto the artwork. The appetite comes from the artwork itself, twelve small loud promises arranged with perfect regularity. The grid is rigid; the contents are wild. That tension is the whole first impression, and the site never lets go of it.

One more thing registers without being read. Each cover carries, along its bottom edge, a small dark strip with a coloured word ("Manga" in purple, "Manhwa" in blue), a star with a number, and an eye with a number. You do not consciously parse these. You absorb that this is a place where things are measured, rated and watched, and that it will tell you the truth about them.

## 2. The choices it made

Every design is a sequence of decisions. These are the ones Atsumaru visibly made, and what each one does to the visitor.

**It made the artwork the only colour.** The interface palette is charcoal, three or four shades of grey, white for headings, and a single restrained lilac used for the one button that matters and the one word that matches your search. Nothing else. The effect is that the covers, which are designed by professional illustrators to grab eyes from a shelf, do precisely that, with no competition from the frame. The site borrows its beauty from its contents and has the humility to admit it.

**It leads with "Hot Updates", not with itself.** The first heading is a statement of recency, and the first content is what changed in the last hour. The visitor is treated as someone who already knows what the site is for and wants to know what is new. Returning readers are rewarded instantly; newcomers learn the site's nature from the material rather than from a description of it.

**It puts the vital numbers on the cover.** Type, rating and view count sit directly on the bottom edge of every image, in a strip dark enough to read against any artwork. Most catalogues hide this on the detail page. Putting it here means a visitor can compare twelve series at a glance without a single click, and the grid becomes a decision surface rather than a gallery.

**It prints relative time under every title.** "6m ago", "19m ago", "3d ago". Not dates. Relative time is a feeling, not a fact; it tells you how warm the page is. A row of titles that all say minutes-ago is a room with people in it.

**It uses one sentence-case voice for every heading.** "Recently Updated", "Most Bookmarked", "Hidden Gems", "Binge-Worthy", "Most Polarizing". Light weight, generous size, no decoration, each followed by the same small chevron. The headings do not shout because the artwork beneath them already does. The restraint in the type is what lets twenty sections stack without fatigue.

**It gives every grid two exits.** At the bottom right of each section sit "See more", a plain word with a downward chevron, and "See all", a small boxed button with a rightward chevron. The first expands the grid in place; the second leaves for the full catalogue. Two verbs, two directions, two visual weights. The visitor is never made to choose between staying and going; both are offered, and the lighter one is the one that keeps you on the page.

**It splits "new" into four moods.** Hot Updates, Recently Updated, Hot Arrivals, Recently Added. Where most catalogues have one shelf called New, Atsumaru distinguishes a chapter landing on a series you follow from a series arriving for the first time, and distinguishes both of those from what the crowd is reacting to. The split costs the visitor nothing, because each section looks identical. It buys a homepage that answers four different questions a reader actually asks.

**It names its shelves like a person, not a database.** "Hidden Gems." "Binge-Worthy." "Most Polarizing." "Spotlight: Sci-Fi." These are the words a well-read friend uses. "Most Polarizing" in particular is an act of confidence: it admits that some of what the site hosts is disputed, and makes the dispute itself a reason to browse.

**It interleaves people with books.** Between the cover grids sit "From the Community", a row of six reader posts with usernames and reputation counts, and "Top Lists", "Hot Lists" and "New Lists", wide cards built from strips of covers with a reader's own title laid over them: "Romance I'll never get to experience", "Switch It Up!", "#lazybum". The page is not a catalogue with a forum bolted on. It is a place where the books and the people who read them share a wall.

**It asks for an account after the first grid, and asks gently.** The pitch comes only after you have seen twelve covers and had a chance to want something. Its heading is a question, "Have we met before?", and its subtitle is a promise about loss, "Create an account to never lose your progress again." Six plain panels with outline icons explain what an account does, in the second person, in complete sentences. Two buttons: a lilac "Create account" and a grey "Login". Beneath them, in small type, "It's completely free and always will be." The whole block has the temperature of a host offering you a coat hook.

**It hides the map until you ask for it.** The site has at least thirty destinations, yet the header shows two icons and a search box. The full map lives behind a two-line menu icon, and opens as a bordered two-column panel that sits over the covers without dimming the page. The visitor who knows where they are going is never shown the whole building; the visitor who wants to explore gets the entire directory in one glance.

**It searches over the page, not away from it.** Pressing the search field opens a centred dialog, dims the page behind, and shows results as you type: a tiny cover, the title with the matched letters in lilac, and one grey line of type, status, year and creator. You never leave. You can see what you were doing through the dimness. Search feels like leaning in, not like walking out.

**It lets a stranger rearrange the home page.** At the very bottom of the page, before the footer, sits a single quiet link: "Customize home". It opens the same "Content preferences" dialog as the sliders icon in the header: toggles for the kinds of comics, for comics versus novels, for how adult the material may be, and controls to exclude genres and rearrange or hide the home sections. This is offered to a visitor with no account. The site trusts you to shape it before it has any idea who you are.

**It strips itself bare when you read.** Open a chapter and the library vanishes. There is the page, tall and centred, black margins either side, and a thin column of seven small icons at the far right. No header, no footer, no logo. The site that spent twenty sections persuading you to read gets out of the way entirely the moment you do.

## 3. The choices it avoided

What a design refuses to do is as legible as what it does, and Atsumaru refuses a great deal.

**No hero.** There is no tall introductory block with a headline, a subheading and a call to action. The site does not explain itself. It would be easy to argue that a newcomer needs orientation; Atsumaru bets instead that twelve covers and the word "Updates" orient faster than any sentence could. The bet pays because the content is self-explanatory. The restraint buys the visitor the first screen for themselves.

**No coloured interface.** Many dark sites tint their surfaces: a navy panel here, a teal accent there, gradients behind headings. Atsumaru's surfaces are colourless. The reward is that colour becomes information. If something on the page is coloured, it is either artwork or the single thing you should do next. Your eye learns the rule within seconds and the rule never lies.

**No permanent sidebar.** A catalogue with sixteen ways to browse would, in most hands, pin a navigation rail to the left edge, and lose a sixth of the screen to it forever. Atsumaru folds the whole directory into a popover and spends the width on covers. Six per row instead of five is the visible dividend.

**No autoplay, no motion for its own sake.** Nothing on the homepage moves until you move. Covers do not slide, headings do not fade in from below, there is no parallax, no cursor trail, no ambient animation. Lower sections reserve their space and quietly fill as you reach them. The page is still, in the way a bookshop is still. Stillness here is not laziness; it is what lets twenty dense sections feel restful instead of relentless.

**No modal on arrival.** No newsletter prompt, no cookie banner, no "allow notifications", no "download the app". The first thing you are allowed to do is look. The site's one interruption, the account pitch, waits until you have scrolled past the first grid, and even then it is inline, part of the page, not a layer on top of it.

**No marketing voice.** The copy never says "discover", "unlock", "seamless" or "experience" in the brochure sense. It says "See more", "See all", "Have we met before?", "It's completely free and always will be." Verbs and plain nouns. The site writes the way a competent person talks, which is why the one place it gets warm, the account pitch, lands as sincerity rather than technique.

**No visual novelty in the cards.** Every cover card is the same: image, badge strip, title, time. No hover flip, no overlay of synopsis, no gradient scrim creeping up from the bottom. The card is a shelf label. The sameness is what lets the grid hold two hundred of them without the visitor feeling the weight.

**No chrome in the reader.** No persistent toolbar across the top, no breadcrumb, no "you might also like" rail beside the page. A column of seven small icons, and the page. The absence is the feature.

What all this refusal buys, in sum, is attention. The site declines to spend any of yours on itself, so that all of it is available for the books.

## 4. The choices it made on purpose

Some of Atsumaru's decisions are not merely good; they are opinionated enough that a committee would have talked them out. These are the ones worth defending.

**Six covers across, two rows, and the titles cut.** Six portrait covers in a row on a desktop screen are small, and the titles beneath are often truncated: "The Seven Deadly Sins: Four Knights of the…", "Shangri-La Frontier: From Trash-Game Hunter to God…". A cautious designer would widen the cards to five and let every title breathe. Atsumaru chooses density, and the choice is right for the audience: readers of serialised comics recognise a series by its cover long before they finish reading its name. The cover is the title. The text is a caption.

**Numbers everywhere.** View counts in the hundreds of thousands, ratings to one decimal, reputation scores beside usernames, upvotes on posts, three counts on every list card. Most consumer design hides metrics because they make the page look like a spreadsheet. Atsumaru shows them because its readers are collectors, and collectors want to know what is popular, what is rated, what is contested. The numbers are set small, grey and consistent, so they read as texture rather than tabulation. Honesty, but at low volume.

**A shelf called "Most Polarizing".** It would be safer to show only the beloved. Instead the site surfaces disagreement as a browsing category, and places it last on the home page, as a kind of dessert. It signals that the site has taste of its own, and that it respects the visitor enough to show them a fight.

**Reputation on display.** Every username on the community cards and the leaderboard carries a count, and the leaderboard shows the day's movement in green and red beside each score. This is a gamified social layer of the kind that often curdles. Here it works because it is pushed down the page and kept small, and because the Rules page, long and plainly written, is explicit that farming reputation and weaponising votes are forbidden. The design shows the score; the copy sets the terms.

**An adult switch in plain sight.** A small segmented control reading "Safe" and "18+" sits at the foot of the menu and again in the footer, with Safe selected by default. Most sites bury this behind an account and a confirmation. Atsumaru treats it as a browsing setting, labelled without euphemism, and pairs it in the preferences dialog with four plainly described content ratings. The frankness is itself a design choice: it tells adults they are being treated as adults, and tells everyone else that the default is clean.

**A tiny keyboard hint in the search box.** Printed faintly inside the search field is a two-key shortcut. Most visitors will never use it. Its presence tells a certain kind of reader, the kind who lives at a keyboard, that this site was built by people like them. It is a wink, and it costs nothing.

**Mixing novels into the comics.** "Recently Updated" shows prose novels and comics side by side, distinguished only by a green "Novel" badge where the others say "Manga" or "Manhwa". Catalogues almost always wall formats apart. Atsumaru decides that what unites the audience is the appetite for serialised story, and lets the badge carry the difference. The home page becomes a single river rather than two canals.

## 5. Every part of the interface, section by section

A walk down the whole page and into the rooms off it.

**The header.** One slim bar, never more. At the left, the name in heavy capitals; it is the only bold text on the page and so becomes an anchor. Two faint icons beside it, a two-line menu and a speech bubble for the community. A wide rounded search field in the exact centre, visually the heaviest element in the bar, because searching is the most common thing anyone does here. At the right, a compass for exploring, sliders for preferences, a chat-service icon, and the two underlined words for logging in. The mood is a well-ordered desk. Its job is to be forgettable until you need it.

**Hot Updates.** The first grid, twelve covers, two rows of six, with the badge strip along each image's bottom edge and a two-line title and a dim time stamp beneath. The job is to show that the library is breathing. The mood is a newsstand at the moment the papers arrive. Beneath it, flush right, the two exits: "See more" and "See all".

**Have we met before?** The account pitch. A question for a heading, one sentence for a sub-heading, six bordered panels in two rows of three, each with an outline icon, a short bold title and one plain sentence. Two buttons centred beneath, one lilac, one grey, and a small reassurance under them. The mood is hospitable, not insistent. Its job is to make a case once and then never again on this page.

**Recently Updated.** The same grid, but mixed: prose novels with green badges beside comics with purple and blue ones. Times here run from "3m ago" upward. The job is to be the most literal pulse on the page. The mood is slightly more motley than Hot Updates, because the covers of self-published novels are rougher than those of licensed comics, and the page lets that roughness show rather than curating it away.

**Popular, Most Bookmarked, Most Talked About.** Three grids that each carry, at the heading's right, a four-way switch: Daily, Weekly, Monthly, All time. The switch is a small dark pill with the active word on a lighter ground. Its job is to let the visitor choose a time horizon without leaving the page. Its mood is instrumental; it looks like a control because it is one.

**Rising, Hot Arrivals, Recently Added, Top Rated, Hidden Gems, Binge-Worthy, Most Polarizing.** Seven further grids, visually identical to the first, differing only in heading and contents. Their job is range: between them they answer "what is climbing", "what just landed", "what is best", "what did I miss", "what can I disappear into", and "what do people fight about". Their mood is the mood of the covers they hold; Top Rated is all classic, confident hand-drawn covers, Hidden Gems is softer and stranger.

**From the Community.** Six wide dark cards in two rows of three. Each shows a small round avatar, a username in colour with a reputation count beside it, a bold post title, a greyed first line of the post, an upvote count at the top right, and a small category tag at the bottom: "Site Feedback", "Off-topic". The cards are quieter than the covers by design; they are grey text on dark grey. The job is to prove there are people here. The mood is a noticeboard in a hallway.

**Spotlight: Sci-Fi.** A themed grid. Its job is editorial: someone chose a genre this week. It is the one place the home page shows a hand on the tiller.

**Top Lists, Hot Lists, New Lists.** Three sections of wide cards, each card a horizontal strip of six or seven cover slivers with the list's name set large and bold in white across the lower left, and beneath, three small counts and the author's name with a tiny avatar. The titles are the readers' own: "Genuine Masterpieces", "A Knights Guide to Romance", "Read on comick/comix", "planned". The job is to show taste as a thing people make. The mood is the most personal on the page; these are other people's shelves, and you can tell.

**Customize home.** A single centred line of grey text with a sliders icon, sitting alone in a band of empty space between the last grid and the footer. Its job is to say, quietly, that all of the above is yours to rearrange. Its placement is the point: it is offered at the end, once you have seen what there is to customise.

**The footer.** A large rounded panel, slightly lighter than the page, holding the name again, the Safe / 18+ switch, and then the whole site map laid out in four columns under small capital labels: ATSUMARU, BROWSE, ACCOUNT, SOCIAL MEDIA. Every entry has an outline icon. Here the directory that the header hid is shown in full, because at the bottom of the page a visitor has run out of content and is ready for a map. The mood is a table of contents at the back of a book.

**The menu.** Opened from the two-line icon, a bordered dark panel drops below the header and sits over the left-hand covers without dimming anything else. Two columns, three groups, hairline dividers, the same outline icons as the footer. Sixteen ways to browse fit in it without crowding. At its foot, three social icons and the Safe / 18+ switch again. The mood is a drawer sliding open: everything in its place, and the room around it unchanged.

**Search.** A centred dialog near the top, the page dimmed behind it. A large field with a magnifier and a close mark, two tabs beneath it, Series and Users, and then flat result rows: a thumbnail, a title with the matched letters turned lilac, and a single grey line of metadata. There is no results page. The dialog is the results. The mood is quick and private, like asking a librarian a question under your breath.

**Content preferences.** A tall centred dialog over a dimmed page, titled plainly, with a close mark at the top right. Three groups of bordered tiles in two columns, each tile a bold label, a one-line plain description and a lilac toggle at the right: the kinds of comics, the two formats, the four content ratings. Below, two further rows for excluded genres and section arrangement, and a lone lilac Save at the foot. Every option is explained in words a stranger would understand. The mood is a settings page that respects you; the job is to make a complex library feel like it has one knob.

**Explore.** The catalogue proper. The same six-across grid, but with a row of five labelled filters above it in small capitals, SEARCH, GENRES, TAGS, TYPE, PUBLISHING STATUS, and beneath that the same four-way time switch plus a sort control. Sixteen sorts are available. The page looks exactly like the home page with the sections stripped out and the controls left in, which is precisely what it is. The mood is workmanlike.

**The series page.** A wide banner of artwork across the top, then the title large and white, then two columns: the cover at left with a stack of full-width buttons beneath it, Chapter 1 in lilac, then Bookmark, Rate, Top posters, Moments in grey, and at right the genres, a cloud of small grey tags, a line of three statistics, a row of small-capital labels (TYPE, STATUS, YEAR, AUTHOR, ARTIST) with their values beneath, and the synopsis as a paragraph of ordinary prose. Below, a chapter list with Overview, All, Read, Unread tabs and a sort toggle. Every fact a reader could want is on one screen, and the single coloured button is the one that starts the book. The mood is a well-made catalogue entry.

**The reader.** Described already, and worth describing again because it is the site's purest moment. Black margins. The page, centred and tall. Seven small icons in a slim column at the right edge: settings, fullscreen, information, home, capture, reload, comments. Nothing else. The job is to disappear. The mood is a cinema after the lights go down.

**Community, Lists, Groups, Leaderboard, Rules.** The rooms off the main hall. Community is a feed with a slim left rail of channels and groups and a three-way switch, Latest, Popular, Following, at the top; posts are cards with a title first, then the author and time on one line. User Lists is a two-column grid of the wide list cards with four sort tabs and four filters. Leaderboard is a single centred table, rank, avatar, name, score, with the day's change in green or red, and a row of tabs for what is being ranked. Rules is a long page of plain prose with a sticky index down the side. All of them share the header, the greys, the type and the icons. None of them looks like a different site.

## 6. The interactivity

Atsumaru's motion vocabulary is small, and almost every word in it means "instantly".

**Cursor.** Ordinary. The pointer becomes a hand over covers, titles, headings and buttons, and a text cursor over the search field. There is no custom cursor, no trail, no magnetic snapping. The cursor is a tool, not a character.

**Hover.** Barely there. Passing over a cover does not flip it, dim it, or raise a synopsis. The site declines the chance to decorate the gesture. What this feels like is reliability: the thing under your hand stays the thing you saw.

**Scroll.** Plain vertical movement, and the page is long, somewhere around fourteen screens. Sections below the fold hold their space and fill as they come into view, so the page never jumps or reflows under you. There is no parallax, no pinned scenes, no elements sliding in from the sides. The scroll feels like turning pages in a thick catalogue: steady, predictable, yours.

**Headings.** Each one is a link. Click "Hot Updates" and you are in the catalogue filtered to that shelf. The chevron beside every heading is the only hint, and it is enough.

**See more.** Expands the grid in place from two rows to more, without leaving the page, and quietly records the expanded state in the address so that a refresh keeps it. The response is immediate. It feels like unfolding a map one more panel.

**See all.** Leaves for the catalogue page, already filtered. Immediate. It feels like walking through a door that was already open.

**The menu.** One click opens it, one click closes it. It drops in place beneath the icon with no ceremony and no dimming. It feels like a drawer, and like a drawer it is fast because it is light.

**Search.** Click the field, or press the two keys printed in it, and the dialog appears with the page dimmed behind. Results arrive as you type, with the matched letters coloured. Closing returns you to exactly where you were, address unchanged. It feels like a thought you had and dismissed.

**The time switches.** Daily, Weekly, Monthly, All time. A small pill where the chosen word sits on a lighter ground. Clicking re-fills the grid beneath. Instrumental, not playful; it feels like adjusting a dial.

**Preferences.** Lilac toggles that slide; a dialog that dims the page; a single Save. The controls feel firm and slightly weighty, which is correct for a decision that reshapes the whole home page.

**Bookmark while logged out.** Clicking the bookmark on a card does not scold you with a popup. It takes you to the sign-up form, remembering where you were so it can return you. The site answers a logged-out gesture with the thing you need to complete it, not with a warning that you cannot.

**The reader.** Clicking the page advances it. A settings panel offers single page or spread, fit or zoom, click behaviour, keyboard shortcuts, and an option to hide the cursor while reading. That last one is the tell: the designers imagined a person who wants even the pointer gone.

Taken together, the interactivity has one personality: it is invisible. Nothing bounces, nothing lingers, nothing wants credit. Every response is the shortest honest answer to the gesture that caused it. The site feels alive not because it moves but because it keeps up.

## 7. What makes it good

The surface reasons are easy to list: dark ground, colourful covers, quiet type, good spacing. They are true and they are not the point. These are the deeper ones.

**It has a single idea and every decision obeys it.** The idea is: the books are the interface. Once that is decided, everything else follows. The palette must be colourless so the covers can be colourful. The type must be light so the covers can be loud. The navigation must hide so the covers can have the width. The cards must be identical so the covers can be different. The motion must be still so the covers can be seen. There is no decision on the site that cannot be traced back to that one sentence, and that is what coherence feels like from the inside: not matching colours, but a shared reason.

**It is dense without being crowded.** Twelve covers, each with three statistics, a title and a time, fit on the first screen with air around them. The density is survivable because of discipline: one type size for titles, one for times, one for headings, one strip for badges, and the same margin everywhere. Density becomes crowding when elements compete; here nothing competes, so the eye can hold a great deal at once. The site trusts its visitors to handle information, and then earns that trust by organising it.

**It is honest at every scale.** The view counts are real and odd: 47.5K, 899.2K, 6.9M. The times are real: 24m, 27m, 3d. The list titles are unedited: "#lazybum", "Slop to read at night". The site does not smooth any of this into marketing copy, and the roughness is exactly what makes it feel inhabited. A page that shows you 899.2K is a page that is not lying about anything else either.

**It has a voice, and the voice is a person.** "Have we met before?" "It's completely free and always will be." "Disagree with the take, not the person." "Every update we ship to Atsumaru, newest first." The release notes are written in full sentences by named people and read like letters. The rules are blunt and funny. The voice is consistent from the home page to the fine print, and that consistency is what makes a visitor feel there is someone home.

**It respects the visitor's time in both directions.** It is fast to use, and it is also fast to leave alone. Nothing nags. The account pitch appears once. The preferences are offered, not pushed. The reader strips itself to nothing. A site that is this easy to ignore is a site you keep coming back to, because it has never once cost you anything.

**It is adhesive because it is alive, not because it is sticky.** The techniques that usually make a site adhesive, infinite feeds, autoplay, notifications, streaks, are absent or kept at arm's length. What brings people back is that the page is visibly different every hour, that the people on it are visibly real, and that nothing about the experience is unpleasant. It earns return visits the way a good café does: by being good, and by being there.

**It is effortless because the effort is all in the editing.** Nothing on the site is technically astonishing. Grids, dialogs, toggles, a reader. The achievement is in what was taken away and in how firmly the remaining pieces hold their places. The site looks as though it was easy, which is the hardest look to achieve.

## 8. What is not good

Admiration is not the same as agreement. These are the places the design misses, rubs, or takes a risk it may not be able to afford.

**The titles are cut, and sometimes cut badly.** Six covers across means the two-line title under each is often truncated with an ellipsis, and for long light-novel names the cut lands mid-phrase: "I Thought I'd Be Popular in a World Where Chastity Is…". For readers who know the series by its cover this is harmless. For a newcomer, or for a series whose cover is text-heavy and in a script they cannot read, the truncated title is the only handle, and it has been shortened to a stub.

**The page is very long and it all looks the same.** Twenty sections of near-identical grids is a feat of consistency, and also a fatigue risk. Below the sixth or seventh grid the headings start to blur: Rising, Hot Arrivals, Recently Added, Hidden Gems. The visual sameness that makes the page restful also makes it hard to know where you are in it. There is no marker of progress, no way to jump to a shelf, and the only map is at the very bottom. A long page with twenty rooms could use a corridor.

**The exits are two but the difference is small.** "See more" and "See all" sit side by side and differ by one word and one chevron direction. The visual weights help, but a visitor moving quickly will click the wrong one from time to time, and the two outcomes, expand here versus leave for the catalogue, are quite different. The pairing is elegant; the labels are too close.

**Hover gives nothing.** The decision not to decorate hover is defensible, and this review has defended it. It also means that a cover offers no preview of what it is about before you commit a click to the series page. A one-line synopsis or a genre on hover would cost almost nothing in stillness and would spare a great many round trips.

**The lowest sections arrive empty.** Because the lower page reserves its space and fills on approach, a visitor who scrolls fast, or whose connection is slow, sees heading after heading over blank dark rectangles. The stillness that serves the page so well up top becomes an awkward silence lower down. The design is right to reserve the space; it could do more to make the waiting look intentional.

**The metadata is inconsistent in small ways.** The search result for one series gives a different year from its own detail page. Some badge strips show a rating and some show only views. Novels have view counts but rarely ratings. None of this is a design flaw exactly, but the design's whole bargain is honesty at small sizes, and small inconsistencies spend that credit.

**The community layer could curdle.** Coloured usernames, badge strings beside names, reputation counts, a public leaderboard with daily deltas. All of it is kept small and pushed down, and the rules are firm, but the visual grammar of status is present on every post card, and status grammars tend to grow. Today it is texture. It will need pruning to stay texture.

**Adult content is a toggle, and the preferences default is generous.** The captured preferences showed Safe, Suggestive and Erotica enabled for a logged-out visitor, with only the most explicit tier off, while the menu switch read Safe. Whatever the intended behaviour, two controls that both appear to govern the same thing, and that can read differently, is a design debt. The frankness is admirable; the overlap is confusing.

**It is a dark site with no light.** There is no light mode, and the greys are tuned for a dim room. In daylight, on a laptop, the contrast between the mid-greys and the charcoal is low, and small grey time stamps on dark grey disappear. The site is beautiful in the conditions it imagines and merely legible outside them.

**It assumes its reader.** The density, the small type, the keyboard hint, the sixteen sorts, the shelf called Most Polarizing: all of it is right for a committed reader of serialised comics, and none of it is explained to anyone else. There is no gentle on-ramp. This is a choice, and this review has praised it, but it is also a wall, and the wall is invisible to the people who built it.
